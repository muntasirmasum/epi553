Logistic Regression Applications

Build, check, and report a logistic regression analysis from start to finish.

Logistic regression
A model-building strategy worked through on BRFSS 2020: Table 1, univariable screening, the multivariable model, confounding, effect modification, fit checks, a forest plot, and a publication-ready paragraph.

NoteLearning objectives

By the end of this lecture you will be able to:

  • follow a seven-step workflow from research question to reported model;
  • screen candidate predictors and build a multivariable logistic model with gtsummary tables;
  • assess confounding with the 10% change-in-estimate rule and effect modification with a likelihood ratio test;
  • check model fit, discrimination, and collinearity before reporting;
  • write a results paragraph whose wording follows the estimates, and avoid common pitfalls.

Introduction

Over the last three lectures we have built up the machinery of logistic regression: the logit link, odds ratios, multiple predictors, Wald and likelihood ratio tests, interactions, goodness-of-fit, discrimination, and the polytomous and ordinal extensions. Today we put it all together.

This is an applications lecture. We will work through a complete analytic workflow, from research question to publication-ready table, the way you would for a manuscript or a thesis chapter.

Part 1: A Model-Building Strategy

There is no single “correct” way to build a regression model. But there is a sensible workflow that most epidemiologists follow:

Seven-Step Logistic Regression Workflow
Step Phase Tools
1 Define the research question DAG, prior literature, study design
2 Univariable screening Simple glm() per predictor; tbl_uvregression()
3 Build the multivariable model glm() with all candidates; tbl_regression()
4 Assess confounding 10% change-in-estimate rule; compare crude vs adjusted
5 Test interactions * operator; LR test; ggpredict()
6 Check fit and assumptions HL test, calibration, ROC/AUC, Cook's D, VIF
7 Report and interpret tbl_regression(); narrative interpretation

Two Schools of Thought

Predictive modeling. Goal: maximize out-of-sample accuracy. Use stepwise selection, LASSO, cross-validation, AUC. The OR is a means to an end.

Etiologic / explanatory modeling. Goal: estimate the unbiased causal effect of an exposure on an outcome. Use a DAG to choose covariates. Confounders are included; mediators and colliders are excluded. The OR for the exposure is the answer.

Most public health research is etiologic. Today we adopt that mindset.

Research Question

Among U.S. adults, is any physical activity or exercise in the past 30 days (outside of work) associated with lower odds of frequent mental distress (FMD), after adjusting for demographic, behavioral, and socioeconomic confounders? Does the association differ by sex?

  • Exposure: exercise (Yes / No)
  • Outcome: fmd (Yes / No)
  • Candidate confounders: age, sex, BMI, sleep hours, income category, smoking status
  • Effect modifier of interest: sex

Step 4: Assess Confounding

The classic 10% change-in-estimate rule: a covariate is a confounder of the exposure-outcome relationship if including it changes the exposure OR by more than 10%.

Show the code
mod_crude <- glm(fmd ~ exercise, data = brfss_logistic, family = binomial)

crude_or <- exp(coef(mod_crude)["exerciseYes"])
adj_or   <- exp(coef(mod_full)["exerciseYes"])
pct_change <- 100 * abs(adj_or - crude_or) / crude_or  # size of the change

tibble(
  Quantity = c("Crude OR (exercise)",
               "Adjusted OR (exercise)",
               "% change in estimate (absolute)"),
  Value    = c(round(crude_or, 3),
               round(adj_or, 3),
               paste0(round(pct_change, 1), "%"))
) |>
  kable(caption = "Change-in-Estimate for Exercise") |>
  kable_styling(bootstrap_options = "striped", full_width = FALSE)
Change-in-Estimate for Exercise
Quantity Value
Crude OR (exercise) 0.7
Adjusted OR (exercise) 0.866
% change in estimate (absolute) 23.7%
TipInterpretation

A change of more than 10% in either direction suggests meaningful confounding by the included covariates; the table reports the size of the change. Here the exercise OR moves from 0.70 (crude) to 0.87 (adjusted), a change of 23.7%, more than 10%, so the covariates confound the association between exercise and FMD. The adjusted OR is closer to 1: part of the crude association reflects differences in age, sex, BMI, sleep, income, and smoking between adults who do and do not exercise. After adjustment the 95% CI for exercise is 0.71 to 1.06, which includes 1. The adjusted OR is the more reliable estimate.

WarningConfounder or mediator?

The etiologic approach in Part 1 leaves mediators out of the model, yet the model above adjusts for sleep hours and BMI. Exercise can improve sleep and lower BMI, so both may lie on the path from exercise to mental distress. Adjusting for a mediator removes part of the effect we want to estimate, so part of the change from the crude OR may not be confounding. Leaving sleep and BMI out (adjusting for age, sex, income, and smoking only) gives an exercise OR of 0.80 (95% CI 0.66 to 0.98). A DAG drawn before the analysis is how to decide which model answers the research question.

Stratum-Specific Estimates

If the interaction is meaningful, report ORs within each level of sex:

Show the code
mod_male <- glm(
  fmd ~ exercise + age + bmi + sleep_hrs + income_cat + smoker,
  data = filter(brfss_logistic, sex == "Male"),
  family = binomial
)

mod_female <- glm(
  fmd ~ exercise + age + bmi + sleep_hrs + income_cat + smoker,
  data = filter(brfss_logistic, sex == "Female"),
  family = binomial
)

strata <- bind_rows(
  tidy(mod_male,   exponentiate = TRUE, conf.int = TRUE) |>
    filter(term == "exerciseYes") |> mutate(stratum = "Male"),
  tidy(mod_female, exponentiate = TRUE, conf.int = TRUE) |>
    filter(term == "exerciseYes") |> mutate(stratum = "Female")
)

# ORs implied by the interaction model (the model the LR test in Step 5 uses)
b <- coef(mod_int)
or_int <- c(Male   = exp(b[["exerciseYes"]]),
            Female = exp(b[["exerciseYes"]] + b[["exerciseYes:sexFemale"]]))

strata |>
  dplyr::select(stratum, estimate, conf.low, conf.high, p.value) |>
  mutate(across(c(estimate, conf.low, conf.high), \(x) round(x, 3)),
         p.value = format.pval(p.value, digits = 3)) |>
  kable(col.names = c("Sex", "OR (Exercise)", "Lower", "Upper", "p"),
        caption = "Stratum-Specific ORs for Exercise") |>
  kable_styling(bootstrap_options = "striped", full_width = FALSE)
Stratum-Specific ORs for Exercise
Sex OR (Exercise) Lower Upper p
Male 1.020 0.729 1.446 0.912
Female 0.784 0.609 1.012 0.060

Here the exercise OR is 1.02 among men and 0.78 among women. These come from separate models for men and women, which let every covariate’s effect differ by sex. The interaction model tested in Step 5 lets only the exercise effect differ; its ORs are 0.96 for men and 0.82 for women. The point estimates differ, but the interaction test found no evidence that the difference is larger than chance, so the single adjusted OR from Step 3 is the main result; the stratum-specific ORs are shown for transparency.

Show the code
fit_stats <- list(
  `McFadden R²`   = round(performance::r2_mcfadden(mod_full)$R2, 3),
  `HL p-value`    = round(hoslem.test(
                      x = as.numeric(brfss_logistic$fmd) - 1,
                      y = fitted(mod_full), g = 10)$p.value, 3),

                      brfss_logistic$fmd, fitted(mod_full),

Part 3: Reporting Results

A Publication-Ready Paragraph

The paragraph below is written from the estimates above; every number and every direction word comes from the model output.

In adjusted models, current smokers had 1.64 times the odds of frequent mental distress compared with former or never smokers (aOR = 1.64; 95% CI: 1.32, 2.02). Each additional hour of sleep was associated with 21% lower odds of FMD (aOR = 0.79; 95% CI: 0.74, 0.84). Exercise was associated with lower odds of FMD before adjustment (crude OR = 0.70), but after adjustment for demographic, behavioral, and socioeconomic covariates the association was attenuated and no longer statistically significant (aOR = 0.87; 95% CI: 0.71, 1.06). The likelihood ratio test for an exercise × sex interaction was not statistically significant (p = 0.431; exercise ORs from the interaction model 0.96 for men and 0.82 for women), so the data provide no evidence that the association with exercise differs by sex.

What Every Logistic Regression Paper Should Report

  1. Sample size and outcome prevalence
  2. Table 1 stratified by outcome
  3. Crude and adjusted ORs with 95% CIs (not just p-values)
  4. Which covariates were included and why (DAG, prior literature, change-in-estimate)
  5. Tests of effect modification that were planned
  6. Goodness-of-fit and discrimination metrics (HL or calibration plot, AUC)
  7. Software and packages with versions

Part 4: Common Pitfalls

Common Pitfalls in Logistic Regression
Pitfall Fix
Reporting p-values without ORs and CIs Always show point estimates with 95% CIs
Stepwise selection on small samples Use a DAG; pre-specify covariates
Adjusting for mediators Draw a DAG; do not adjust on the causal pathway
Adjusting for colliders Avoid conditioning on common effects of exposure and outcome
Ignoring the events-per-variable rule (EPV < 10) Aim for ≥ 10 events per predictor; use penalized methods if not
Interpreting OR as relative risk when outcome is common Use log-binomial or modified Poisson when prevalence > ~10%
Forgetting to check linearity in the logit Restricted cubic splines or LOWESS-of-logit plots
Reporting only the p-value of an interaction Always show stratum-specific estimates

Summary

Step Key Action
1. Question Anchor your model in a clear research question and DAG
2. Screen Univariable models inform but do not select
3. Build Include confounders identified a priori
4. Confound Compare crude vs adjusted; 10% rule of thumb
5. Modify Plan interactions; report stratum-specific estimates
6. Check Fit, calibration, discrimination, diagnostics
7. Report ORs + CIs, narrative interpretation, full transparency
